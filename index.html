Cloud Cost Forecasting

How cloud costs change as a business scales, and why the bill rose in steps rather than with demand.

ForecastingCost modellingSynthetic data
Summary What I checked first What the bill was made of The model Cost per user Data vs assumptions

Summary

I wanted to forecast how cloud costs would change as the user base grew. The obvious approach was to tie the bill to demand, but the history didn’t support that. Most of the bill was fixed, and it went up in steps whenever more capacity was needed.

So I built the forecast around those steps. It works out which infrastructure size each level of usage needs, prices it, and adds a small usage-based cost on top.

What I checked first

I put the monthly cloud bill next to leads, enrolments and revenue. Leads moved a lot from month to month, about six times between the quietest and busiest months, while the bill stayed almost flat.

A model like cost per lead or cost as a share of revenue assumes the bill grows with demand. The data showed it didn’t, so I dropped that approach.

What the bill was made of

I split the bill into two parts:

  • Fixed cost: servers, databases, storage and other infrastructure that runs no matter how many people use the platform.
  • Variable cost: things that move with usage, such as data transfer.

Most of it was fixed, which explained why the bill hardly reacted to demand. The forecast starts from the fixed cost and treats the variable part separately.

The model

My first try was a fixed base plus a cost per user. That gives a straight line, with a little more cost for every new user. The history looked different: the bill stayed flat, then jumped when the setup ran out of room.

So I modelled the infrastructure rather than the cost. For each month, the model checks:

  • how many users are active
  • how many are on at the busiest point
  • which infrastructure size that needs
  • what that size costs, plus the small usage-based part

The cost only changes when usage passes a size’s limit, so going from 10,000 to 20,000 users doesn’t double the bill. The forecast shows when the bigger increases are likely to happen.

Cost per user

Dividing the total cost by the number of users shows there isn’t one fixed cost per user. For example, with a fixed cost of $20,000 a month:

UsersCost per user
20,000$1.00
200,000$0.10
2,000,000$0.01

The bill is the same in every row. It’s just shared across more users, which is why a single cost-per-user figure would be misleading.

Data vs assumptions

From historical data

  • the starting fixed cost
  • the split between fixed and variable cost

Assumptions

  • how big each future infrastructure step will be
  • what share of users are active at the busiest point

Both assumptions are inputs, so they can be changed without rebuilding the model.

The dataset is synthetic and was made for this case study. The numbers are invented; only the shape of the cost behaviour is meant to be realistic.

Lead Classification from Sales Chats

Sorting hundreds of sales chats a day by buying intent, so agents call the students most likely to buy first.

Lead scoringRule-based classificationProduction
Summary The problem Why it was hard What each chat gets Why rules, not AI How it works Results What I’d check next

Summary

Leads from every ad and channel landed in one chat inbox, and agents had no quick way to tell who was serious. I built a rule-based classifier that tags every chat by buying intent and refreshes the call list several times a day, so the students most likely to buy are reached first.

  • 28K+ chats tagged over five months
  • High-intent leads were about a quarter of qualified leads and brought around 60% of their sales
  • Fewer than 0.1% of chats tagged irrelevant ever led to a sale
  • Conversion on the main course rose from 1% to 4% in four months

Built with Python, Claude Code, the Chatwoot API and Google Sheets.

The problem

Many leads came from ads that open a chat with a message already typed out. The student sends it, a bot asks a few questions, and then a sales agent takes over.

Some students never wrote again. Some were years away from the exam, wanted a course the business didn’t offer, or were looking for a job. A few were asking about fees, start dates and how to pay, and those were the ones agents needed to reach.

Before this, anyone who replied counted as qualified, so “ok” counted the same as “how do I pay?”. Agents opened chats one by one, and students who were ready to buy waited behind chats that were never going to sell.

Why it was hard

  • Nothing to check against. Nobody had tagged these chats before, so in the first week I tagged close to 100 a day by hand to learn what “right” looked like.
  • Messy text. Students mixed languages and spelled words however they liked, and even agents wrote “irrelevant” several ways. Lead-scoring tools rely on forms and website tracking, and all we had was the chat.
  • Live use. Agents worked from the output during the day, so a bad rule could reach the call list within hours. Every fix also had to be re-run on past days without quietly changing last week’s numbers.

What each chat gets

Every chat gets a tag, a sub-label, a confidence level and a one-line reason.

TagMeans
QualifiedA real student in a real conversation, ranked high, medium or low intent, or can’t afford
No responseSent the pre-typed message and nothing else
IrrelevantNot a lead for this course
Future leadA real student, but for a later exam year

Sales used the tag to decide who to call, and marketing used the sub-label to see why leads were lost. Payment is a separate flag, so a chat can be high intent and paid at the same time.

Why rules, not AI

I drafted an AI version with the same labels first, then went with rules because of how often it had to run and how quickly mistakes needed fixing.

  • Free to run, so tagging several times a day added no API bill
  • Re-running every past day after a fix took seconds
  • The same chat always got the same tag, which daily reports depend on
  • A wrong tag pointed straight to the rule behind it

The cost is upkeep. Students keep finding new ways to say the same thing, so the rules keep growing, and rules can’t read voice notes or catch sarcasm.

How it works

  1. Prepare the chat. Pull the full thread, mark who sent each message and remove the pre-typed opening. Each day’s tag uses only that day’s messages.
  2. Apply the rules in order. Chats that aren’t leads are set aside first. The agent’s call comes first, and “not interested” only counts near the end of a chat.
  3. Rank by buying intent. What a student asks matters more than how much they write, so “too expensive” followed by “how do I pay?” is high intent.
  4. Correct and de-duplicate. Hand corrections override the rules, and each student keeps only their best tag per course.

During the day it tagged new chats several times and pushed high-intent leads to the sheet agents already used. Once a day it re-tagged everything as the final record.

To improve the rules, I compared them with hand-tagged chats, logged each mismatch with the chat that caused it, fixed the rule and re-ran past days to see what else changed. I confirmed every proposed fix by hand before it went in.

Results

Agents started each round with the students most likely to buy, instead of working through the inbox in the order chats arrived. Because the list refreshed during the day, those students were reached the same day they messaged.

  • High-intent leads were 27% of qualified leads and brought 61% of their sales, converting 3 to nearly 9× better than the other tiers. Part of that comes from being called first, which was the point.
  • Fewer than 0.1% of chats set aside as irrelevant ever led to a sale, so agents could skip them safely.
  • “Can’t afford” leads stayed on the list and converted better than medium and low intent.
  • Conversion on the main course went from 1% to 4% in four months. Lead volume fell over the same period as the season ended, so not all of that is the tagging.

What I’d check next

  • Accuracy. The rules were checked against hand-tagged chats but never on a separate test set. Next is a labelled test set with precision and recall for each tag, compared side by side with an AI classifier.
  • Future leads. On the main course they converted at 8.1%, higher than qualified leads. Either next-year students signed up on schedule, or the “next year” rules were catching students who were ready now.

Company details and example phrases are generalised. The labels, rules, workflow and results are from production.
