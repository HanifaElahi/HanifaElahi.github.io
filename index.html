Cloud Cost Forecasting

How cloud costs change as a business scales, and why the bill rose in steps rather than with demand.

ForecastingCost modelingSynthetic data
Summary What I checked first What the bill was made of The model Cost per user Data vs assumptions

Summary

I wanted to forecast how cloud costs would change as the user base grew. The obvious approach was to tie the bill to demand, but the history didn’t support that. Most of the bill was fixed, and it went up in steps whenever more capacity was needed.

So I built the forecast around those steps. It works out which infrastructure size each level of usage needs, prices it, and adds a small usage-based cost on top.

What I checked first

I put the monthly cloud bill next to leads, enrolments and revenue. Leads moved a lot from month to month, about six times between the quietest and busiest months, while the bill stayed almost flat.

A model like cost per lead or cost as a share of revenue assumes the bill grows with demand. The data showed it didn’t, so I dropped that approach.

What the bill was made of

I split the bill into two parts:

  • Fixed cost: servers, databases, storage and other infrastructure that runs no matter how many people use the platform.
  • Variable cost: things that move with usage, such as data transfer.

Most of it was fixed, which explained why the bill hardly reacted to demand. The forecast starts from the fixed cost and treats the variable part separately.

The model

My first try was a fixed base plus a cost per user. That gives a straight line, with a little more cost for every new user. The history looked different: the bill stayed flat, then jumped when the setup ran out of room.

So I modelled the infrastructure rather than the cost. For each month, the model checks:

  • how many users are active
  • how many are on at the busiest point
  • which infrastructure size that needs
  • what that size costs, plus the small usage-based part

The cost only changes when usage passes a size’s limit, so going from 10,000 to 20,000 users doesn’t double the bill. The forecast shows when the bigger increases are likely to happen.

Cost per user

Dividing the total cost by the number of users shows there isn’t one fixed cost per user. For example, with a fixed cost of $20,000 a month:

UsersCost per user
20,000$1.00
200,000$0.10
2,000,000$0.01

The bill is the same in every row. It’s just shared across more users, which is why a single cost-per-user figure would be misleading.

Data vs assumptions

From historical data

  • the starting fixed cost
  • the split between fixed and variable cost

Assumptions

  • how big each future infrastructure step will be
  • what share of users are active at the busiest point

Both assumptions are inputs, so they can be changed without rebuilding the model.

The dataset is synthetic and was made for this case study. The numbers are invented; only the shape of the cost behaviour is meant to be realistic.
